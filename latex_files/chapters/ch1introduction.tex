\section{Problem Statement}
The \ac{DIT}, a theory that has been gaining traction in the 2020s, especially after the acquisition of Twitter by Elon Musk, is a conspiracy theory that asserts that the activity of the internet is composed mainly of bots \cite{DIT}. A bot is defined as “an autonomous program on the internet or another network that can interact with systems or users”. In other words, a bot, if programmed correctly, can mimic a user on the internet and portray themselves as a human behind the account. This can lead to bot accounts interacting with normal human accounts and potentially other bot accounts, fueling the idea behind the \ac{DIT}. This can introduce financial concerns such as content creators experiencing less growth or companies choosing where to advertise. For example, Twitch, a large online streaming platform owned by Amazon, has consistently dealt with view-botting and has given directions on how streamers can handle the situation if they suspect that their channels are being view-botted \cite{TwitchGuidelines}. View-botting, explained by the twitch moderation team, can be harmful towards streamers because it disallows them to grow their channels organically. This can prevent them from obtaining opportunities in the future. There are also societal concerns such as the spread of misinformation and the skewing of public opinion through bot activity.

This project aims to look at \ac{AI}, \ac{LLM}, and more specifically, \ac{GPT}, and what roles they play in the creation of bot accounts, if any. We will also try to create bot accounts of our own to explore the complexities and limitations regarding the creation of bot accounts, as well as the motivations behind them. The main purpose of this project is to see how bot activity can influence the political climate on social media platforms, specifically X, formerly known as Twitter. We will collect data that X allows us to, such as impressions, engagement, and replies. 


\section{History and Background}
Bots have regularly been used by companies and government entities on a variety of different platforms, such as phone hot-lines to California's Medical benefits, virtual assistants on all the latest smart phones, and chat bots on company websites with automated responses to help with common questions. They are useful in all activities where human responses would be necessary. Normally, these bots are easy to detect due to their limited automated responses, but with the advancement of technologies such as \ac{ML}, \ac{LLM}s, and \ac{GPT}s, bots are now able to pass the Turing test \cite{LLMTuringTest}. 

The Turing test is a test created by Alan Turing in which a human interrogates two witnesses on a text-only interface. One witness is a human and the other is a machine or a bot imitating a human. The interrogator then decides which witness is the real human. This is also known as imitation game. If a human cannot tell the difference between a human and a bot a significant number of times, then the bot is said to have passed the Turing test. With the introduction of \ac{GPT}s and their quick advancement, GPT-4 was said to have been judged as human 54\% of the time in comparison to the actual human, who were identified 67\% of the time \cite{LLMBotGuard}. However, just a month after the publication of J. Duan et al \cite{LLMBotGuard}, the next iteration of GPT technology, GPT-4.5, passed the Turing test \cite{LLMTuringTest}. 

Because social media has a massive reach and millions of users are on social media apps on a daily basis, it is important to be able to detect bots on these platforms, especially when hate speech is seen to increase on platforms like X \cite{BerkeleyResearch}. Since Elon Musk's purchase of X, hate speech has increased by about 50\% through May 2023. In addition to this, the presence of bots likely did not decrease after Musk's purchase \cite{BerkeleyResearch}. However, other studies have shown a decrease in bot activity from 2016 to 2024 in regards to the time periods leading up to the presidential elections. In their study, K. Debanath, S. Aich, and A. Srizon found that there was a "76\% reduction of detected bot prevalence compared to 2016 levels (2.22\% vs. 9.7\%)" \cite{BotActivity}. This does not mean there is less bot activity on the site but instead, bots may be harder to detect.
